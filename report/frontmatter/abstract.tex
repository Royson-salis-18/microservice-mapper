\begin{center}{\bfseries\Large ABSTRACT\par}\end{center}
\vspace{4pt}
\begin{spacing}{1.25}\small
A microservice failure spreads. A slow datastore stalls the services that query it, their callers time out, retries pile on more load, and within a minute the operator faces dozens of alarms. Severity alone cannot separate the originating fault from its victims. Recent diagnosis methods localise the fault with graph neural networks over fused metrics, traces and logs. They need labelled failures and GPU inference, and the engineer cannot audit what they return.

This project builds the opposite: an unsupervised pipeline that runs on one CPU core and watches Docker containers from outside the application, over SSH. It reports a cause, a forecast of what fails next, a fault type and a runbook. The platform (about 36\,000 lines of TypeScript, 339 automated tests) recovers the call graph from container socket tables, draws it in 2D and 3D, and runs five components. A cascade model learns per-edge propagation probabilities online as Beta--Binomial posteriors. A load-aware model of normal removes the workload that all services share, so a change in traffic does not read as a fault. A hybrid detector combines that model with a robust rule layer and treats container facts (stopped, crash-looping) as evidence, not as degrees of anomaly. A ranker separates a service's own deviation from what its neighbours explain. Call directions come from telemetry. A Detection engines page runs the Rules, ML and Hybrid engines side by side on the same readings.

The first ML engine, a per-service Isolation Forest, flagged every service when traffic stopped. On fault-free recordings where only the load moved, it sat in alarm for 23 to 52\% of the time, and the four other detectors we tried did no better. We compared nine unsupervised models of normal on injected faults and on those recordings. The adopted load-aware residual model stays in alarm for 0\% of them and matches the others on injected faults. The evaluation covers 62 faults injected blind into two real containerised systems (217 minutes each), 84 faults in live campaigns, and 178 held-out synthetic faults over 16 topologies, with bootstrap intervals, paired tests, an ablation and an amplification study. The hybrid names the true cause first for 71\% of real faults against 63\% for the rule layer; the paired difference ($+8.1$ points, 95\% CI $[-1.6,+17.7]$) is not significant. Learned propagation calibrates better than fixed edge probabilities (ECE 0.033 against 0.051 on 69 observed cascades). Learned directions stay correct when service names carry no information (27 of 31 pairs, against none for the name rule). Change-point alarms cut the median detection delay on the real systems from 11.3\,s to 4.8\,s. One decision window costs about 0.37\,ms per service on one core. The report also lists what failed, two measurement bugs that flattered the engines, and findings from operating the benchmark hosts.
\end{spacing}
