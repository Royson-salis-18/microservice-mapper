\begin{center}{\bfseries\Large ABSTRACT\par}\end{center}
\vspace{4pt}
\begin{spacing}{1.25}\small
When a microservice fails, the failure does not stay where it starts. A slow datastore drags down the services that query it, their callers time out, retries amplify the load, and within a minute the operator faces dozens of alarms in which the originating fault cannot be told from its victims by severity alone. Recent diagnosis methods localise the fault with graph neural networks over fused metrics, traces and logs; they need labelled failures, GPU inference, and give an output the on-call engineer cannot audit.

This project builds the alternative as a complete, unsupervised, single-core pipeline that observes Docker containers from outside the application, over SSH, and assists the operator with a cause, a prediction of what fails next, a fault type and a runbook. A platform of about 36\,000 lines of TypeScript and 339 automated tests recovers the call graph from container socket tables, shows it in 2D and 3D, and runs the diagnosis. Its core is a probabilistic cascading-failure model whose per-edge propagation probabilities are learned online as Beta--Binomial posteriors; a load-aware model of normal, which explains away the workload every service shares so that a change of traffic is not mistaken for a fault, and a hybrid detector in which it and a robust rule layer jointly raise the alarm, with container facts (stopped, crash-looping) treated as evidence rather than degree; a ranker that separates a service's own deviation from what its neighbours explain; and call directions learned from telemetry. A Detection engines page runs the Rules, ML and Hybrid engines in parallel and shows what each detected, computed and predicted.

The first ML engine, a per-service Isolation Forest, raised an alarm on every service when the traffic stopped; on fault-free recordings in which only the load moved it was in alarm for 23 to 52\% of the time, and so were the other four detectors tried. Nine unsupervised models of normal were compared on injected faults and on those recordings; the adopted load-aware residual model is in alarm for 0\% of them at the same accuracy on injected faults. The evaluation uses 62 faults injected blind into two real containerised systems over 217 minutes each, 84 faults in live campaigns, and 178 held-out synthetic faults over 16 topologies, with bootstrap intervals, paired tests, an ablation and an amplification study. The hybrid names the true cause first for 71\% of real faults against 63\% for the rule layer (paired difference $+8.1$ points, 95\% CI $[-1.6,+17.7]$, not significant); learned propagation is better calibrated than fixed edge probabilities (ECE 0.033 against 0.051) on 69 observed cascades; learned directions stay correct when service names carry no information (27 of 31 pairs against none); and change-point alarms cut the median detection delay on the real systems from 11.3\,s to 4.8\,s. A decision window costs about 0.37\,ms per service on one core. The report also gives what did not work, two measurement bugs that made the engines look better than they were, and operational findings from the benchmark hosts.
\end{spacing}
