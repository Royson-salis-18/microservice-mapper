\section{Background}
Large applications now split into many small services that talk over the network. A shop runs separate services for catalogue, cart, payment, shipping and orders, each with its own database or cache \cite{dragoni2017microservices}. Teams deploy and scale each piece alone. That freedom has a price: a fault no longer stays where it starts.

Take a slow database. Its client waits, so it answers slowly. The clients of \emph{that} service time out, retry and add load on top of load. A dozen services alarm within a minute, and the on-call engineer faces a wall of red in which the first failure is no louder than its victims. Practitioners call this an alert storm. The alerts themselves cost little; deciding which one deserves action costs hours \cite{beyer2016sre,notaro2021survey}.

Two facts make the problem tractable. Trouble follows the call graph, from a callee to the services that wait on it. And it follows the graph \emph{probabilistically}: a caller with a timeout, a cache or a circuit breaker can survive a failing dependency, while one without them cannot. A tool that knows the graph and the chance that trouble crosses each edge can answer what a ranked list of alarms cannot. Which service started it? Which healthy services will fall next?

Recent research answers the first question with heavy machinery: models that fuse metrics, traces and logs and localise the fault with graph neural networks \cite{zhang2023diagfusion,sun2024deephunt,lee2023eadro}. They work. They also demand labelled failures that most teams never collect, accelerated hardware at inference time, and they return output (an embedding ranking, a cluster number) that an engineer cannot check against the dashboard. Classic observability products take the opposite trade. They show nothing until someone adds client libraries, agents or a sidecar to every pod.

This project watches the containers \emph{from outside}, over SSH and the Docker socket, and modifies nothing. The observations feed a live dependency graph. An unsupervised, single-core pipeline on that graph proposes a root cause, a forecast of what fails next, a fault type and a short runbook, each backed by numbers the operator can inspect.

\section{Problem Statement}
The project addresses four linked problems that a team running containerised microservices meets in practice:

\begin{itemize}
\item Invisible dependencies. The real call graph of a running system is rarely documented, and it drifts. Compose files list what was \emph{declared}, not what is actually talking. Without instrumentation, nobody can say which service calls which, or in which direction.
\item Alert storms. When one service fails, its callers fail too. Ranking alarms by severity points at the loudest service, which is often a victim and not the cause.
\item No view of what comes next. Operators learn that a service is affected only after it alarms. A model of how failure spreads on this particular system could warn earlier.
\item Black-box diagnosis. Methods that need training data and return an opaque score are hard to trust at three in the morning. An engineer needs to see the evidence and how it was combined.
\end{itemize}

The problem is therefore to \emph{observe a containerised system without touching it, recover its call graph, detect trouble per service against that service's own normal, rank the likely origin of an incident, predict how it will spread, and show every step in a form an engineer can verify}. It has to be solved with no labelled data and no specialised hardware.

\section{Motivation and Need for the Project}
Small teams and student projects run the systems that heavyweight AIOps products serve badly: a few Docker hosts on a cloud account, no budget for per-host licences, applications nobody wants to re-instrument. These systems still fail in cascades. We watched it on our own benchmarks: killing one datastore container raised alarms on its callers within seconds.

Three observations shaped the work. The host already shows most of what an operator needs: container state, CPU and memory against the container's own limit, network counters and each container's table of open TCP sockets. A per-service model of ``normal'' replaces global thresholds, which fail because services differ by orders of magnitude in every metric. And a result nobody can reproduce or audit is worth little, so the evaluation had to meet the standard of the tool: ground truth the detector never sees, confidence intervals, paired tests and an honest list of failures.

\section{Objectives}
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*.]
\item To build a platform that connects to Docker hosts over SSH, discovers the service topology, and shows live health, metrics and dependencies in a 2D and a 3D view, without changing the monitored applications.
\item To recover call \emph{directions} from observed sockets and, where names carry no information, from the services' own behaviour.
\item To detect trouble per service against its own recent history with a hybrid of a load-aware learned model and a robust statistical rule layer, treating container facts such as ``stopped'' or ``crash-looping'' as evidence rather than degree.
\item To rank the root cause of an incident by separating a service's own deviation from what its neighbours explain, and by running a random walk over the call graph.
\item To model cascading failure with learned per-edge propagation probabilities, predict which services are next, and warn early about gradual faults.
\item To classify the fault type and attach a short runbook, so the output is something an engineer can act on.
\item To make the three engines (Rules, ML, Hybrid) switchable and to show, side by side and on the same readings, what each one detects, computes and predicts.
\item To evaluate all of it on real and synthetic fault campaigns with ground truth the detector never sees, reporting confidence intervals, paired tests, an ablation and the negative results.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope of the Project}
The system targets Docker-based microservice stacks on Linux hosts that can be reached over SSH. It reads container state, statistics and socket tables; it does not read application payloads and it does not require code changes. It was developed and measured on the following applications:

\begin{itemize}
\item ShopFlow, a 13-service retail checkout system and \textbf{LedgerLine}, a 12-service retail-banking transfer system. Both were built for this study as test benches, each on its own EC2 host with its own Fault Lab and a Prometheus--Grafana--Loki monitoring stack (Section~\ref{sec:benches}); both were run under open-loop traffic for 217 minutes with 62 faults injected;
\item Sock Shop, the open microservices demo, used for development;
\item a social-network application from the DeathStarBench suite \cite{gan2019deathstar} (27 containers) and the OpenTelemetry demo, used to check that discovery works on third-party systems;
\item a synthetic generator that writes runs in the same recorded format over 16 random topologies, used to enlarge the evaluation.
\end{itemize}

The mapper is not a distributed tracing tool: there are no request identifiers or spans, and the ``Traces'' page shows sampled TCP connection snapshots. It does not replace an application performance monitoring suite. No language model takes part in the diagnosis; the ``assistant'' is a transparent composition of statistical models, and Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} discusses where a language-model agent could be added.

\section{Significance and Expected Contribution}
The project shows that useful root-cause analysis does not need instrumentation, labels or a GPU, and it measures how far that goes. Its contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item an \textbf{instrumentation-free observation layer} that recovers topology and call direction from container sockets and Docker state;
\item a \textbf{probabilistic cascading-failure model} whose per-edge propagation probabilities are Beta--Binomial posteriors learned online from the system's own incidents, with risk flowing over the graph by noisy-OR;
\item a \textbf{load-aware model of normal} that explains away the workload every service shares and judges only what is left, so a change of traffic is not mistaken for a fault (it replaced a per-service Isolation Forest that was in alarm for most of a load test), and a \textbf{hybrid detector} in which that model and a robust rule layer jointly raise the alarm and container facts count as evidence, with an operating contract that reports alarm \emph{time} as well as false-alarm episodes;
\item a \textbf{fact-aware, decomposition-based ranker} for the root cause, and \textbf{call directions learned from telemetry};
\item a \textbf{Detection engines page} that runs Rules, ML and Hybrid in parallel and shows what each detected, computed and predicted;
\item a \textbf{reproducible evaluation} with a blind fault injector, a held-out synthetic benchmark, bootstrap intervals, paired tests, an ablation and an amplification study, together with the results that did not help and a measurement bug that had hidden half of the real faults;
\item a working \textbf{platform} of about 36\,000 lines of TypeScript with 339 automated tests, a traffic generator and a host-check tool, used throughout the experiments.
\end{itemize}
