\subsection{Which detector}
Figure~\ref{fig:roc} and Table~\ref{tab:roc} score each detector as a service-level classifier. The result is not what a learned-detector paper would hope for: the plain rule $z$-score is as good as or better than every learned detector on discrimination (AUROC 0.696 against 0.635 for the Isolation Forest on the real systems; 0.873 against 0.862 on the synthetic set), and Mahalanobis is the best learned detector on the synthetic set (0.868). Separation on the real systems is weak for every detector (AUROC 0.62 to 0.70), because many real faults hit services that are nearly idle.

The Isolation Forest is therefore kept for what it adds to the hybrid, not for discrimination alone. It is the quietest detector (alarm time of 5\% against 9\% for the rules on the real systems, Table~\ref{tab:main}); it separates own from symptom evidence; and its alarms complement those of the rules, so their union detects more faults than either (92\% against 89\% on the synthetic set). The ensemble of four detectors was not run on the synthetic set to completion and is reported for the real systems only.

\subsection{Detection delay and root-cause depth}
Figure~\ref{fig:delay} shows detection delay as a cumulative distribution. On the real systems the change-point alarm lifts the detected fraction from 90\% to 95\% and moves the median from 10.5\,s to 4.9\,s; on the synthetic set, whose faults are abrupt, the curves almost coincide. It does so at a price visible in Table~\ref{tab:main}: alarm time rises from 7\% to 16\% on the real systems. Figure~\ref{fig:atk} shows the true cause in the top $k$: the ML-based rankers reach it first more often than the rules on the real systems, and the curves converge by $k = 2$ to $3$ near the 90\% detection ceiling, so the remaining misses are mostly faults the engine does not see (brownouts on near-idle services) rather than faults it ranks badly.

\subsection{Ablation}
\label{sec:ablation}
Figure~\ref{fig:abl} removes one mechanism at a time. The most important single component is the \code{docker stop} fact: without it \cn{} falls by 19 points on the real systems and 16 on the synthetic set, because every stopped service goes unnamed. The crash-loop fact (3 and 7 points), the range guard (4 and 11 points) and the random walk (3 and 1 points) each contribute. Two mechanisms act on the contract rather than on accuracy. Removing the one-sided features raises alarm time on the real systems from 5\% to 30\%. Alarming at level one instead of two raises it to 22\% and false alarms to 11.3 per hour while changing \cn{} by only $+2$ points. The one-sided features do not matter on the synthetic set, where restarts do not lower memory: an effect of the real systems that the simulator does not reproduce.

\subsection{When does the graph matter? The amplification study}
Standard benchmarks have faults whose own service is also the loudest, which is the case where ranking by severity cannot fail. To probe the regime in which it can, soft faults (brownout, latency, memory) were regenerated with the amplification factor $A \in \{1, 1.5, 2, 3, 4\}$, the hybrid's alarms were kept identical for every ranker, and only the ranking varied (Figure~\ref{fig:amp}). As $A$ grows, the share of faults in which some victim is louder than the true cause rises from 15\% to 25\% (panel c), the severity ranking falls from 67\% to 62\%, and the walk holds near 67\%, so the gain of the walk over severity grows from about one point to about five (panel b). Its intervals include zero at every level ($+4.7$ points, 95\% CI $[-0.9, +11.3]$, at $A = 4$). The strongest ranker on soft faults throughout is the own-versus-explained decomposition used by the rule layer (69 to 75\%; $+5.7$ to $+9.4$ points over severity, with intervals excluding zero at $A = 1.5$ and $A = 4$). A router that uses it whenever no container fact holds did not change the full benchmarks (within $\pm 2$ points), so the shipped ranking is unchanged. Graph-aware ranking is a modest, regime-dependent gain, and the decomposition matters more than the walk.

\subsection{The cascade model}
Figure~\ref{fig:cal} evaluates who-is-next predictions at the first alarm of each fault: for every service still healthy, the probability that it turns unhealthy before the fault ends. On the synthetic set (159 faults, 1\,196 predictions, 80 cascades) the learned propagation model reaches AUROC 0.847 against 0.784 for the same noisy-OR with every edge fixed at 0.7, a Brier score of 0.055 against 0.060 and an ECE of 0.039 against 0.058. The reliability curve is noisy in the upper bins, which hold few predictions. \emph{On the real systems the experiment cannot be done well:} only 5 of 420 predictions were followed by a cascade (54 faults), so the real-system comparison (AUROC 0.685 against 0.661; the live mapper's own risk values 0.530) is not informative. The real faults are mostly single-service outages that the systems absorb; whether learned propagation helps on a real cascade is untested.

\subsection{Learned directions}
Figure~\ref{fig:dirs} reports direction accuracy on observed pairs that are true calls. With names available the name rule is correct for 21 of 31 real pairs (and leaves 10 undecided); the learned rule is correct for 28 of 31 (3 wrong). With service names hidden the name rule is undecided for every pair while the learned rule is unchanged; the same holds on the synthetic set (95 of 204 against 197 of 204 correct). The effect on ranking is smaller than the effect on directions. With names available the learned directions do \emph{not} beat the name rule (71\% against 74\% on the real systems, $-3.2$ points, not significant). With names hidden they reach 71\% against 69\% for the name rule and 73\% for the true graph. Learned directions are a robustness mechanism, not an accuracy gain.

\subsection{Early warning and fault diagnosis}
The trend forecast warns before the alarm for 16 of 178 synthetic faults (7 of 18 memory leaks) with a median lead of 30\,s and about one false warning per fault-free hour (Figure~\ref{fig:early}); on the real systems it fired for one of 62 faults, since those faults are abrupt. Figure~\ref{fig:diag} shows fault-type diagnosis. With the true service the forest is correct for 99\% of synthetic faults 60\,s after the alarm; on the engine's own suspect it is 77\%, because a wrong suspect is rarely typed correctly. On the real systems a hand-written decision list generalises best (59\% on the suspect, 84\% on the true service), because real flaps are pause cycles and real brownouts on idle services show no throttling: differences between the simulator and the real systems that the learned models absorb as synthetic-specific patterns.

\subsection{Cost}
The pipeline is unsupervised, needs no GPU and runs on one core. In a single replay of the two real systems (12 and 13 services) one decision window, covering the per-service forests, facts, random walk and cascade risk, took about 6 to 7\,ms on average, far below the 5\,s collection period. How the cost grows with the number of services beyond these sizes was not measured, so no scaling claim is made: the per-service forests are independent and the walk is linear in edges times iterations, but this was not benchmarked.

\subsection{Operational findings from the benchmark hosts}
\label{sec:hostfindings}
Operating the benchmark hosts produced three findings that changed the tools.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{A host too small for its workload looks like a mapper fault.} The OpenTelemetry demo ran on a 2-vCPU, 4\,GiB instance. The check tool measured a load average of about 25, 3.6 of 3.8\,GiB of memory in use, no swap, and docker did not answer within 25 seconds; the dashboard showed no services. A second host with 4\,GiB of swap and a load average of 0.2 ran 27 containers of a social-network benchmark normally. The lesson is operational: the mapper depends on the host's Docker daemon answering, and a host that cannot answer shows nothing. The check tool now says so in one step.
\item \textbf{Idle front ends have no edges.} On the healthy social-network host, the front-end containers showed no outgoing sockets when sampled, because nothing was calling them; an undeclared edge expires ten minutes after its last traffic. A front end that is idle therefore has no edge until traffic arrives, which is correct behaviour but looks like a fault. Declared edges from the Compose file keep an idle link on the screen.
\item \textbf{Hard-coded project names blocked traffic.} The traffic controller accepted only five named projects, so benchmark hosts with other names could not be driven. Unknown projects now get a generic workflow.
\end{itemize}


\section{Live Fault Lab Experiments: Auto-restart On and Off}
\label{sec:live}
The 62-fault evaluation above was made on recorded campaigns. To test the mapper once more against a system it cannot see into, and to settle whether its optional auto-restart changes the results, both benchmark benches (Section~\ref{sec:benches}) were run live inside Docker, reached by the mapper over SSH exactly like a remote host (auto-restart exists only on that path). The hybrid engine decided the incidents and the harness scored them against the injector's private ledger. Only one bench ran at a time. The run scripts and every recorded file are in the repository (\texttt{run/scripts/autorestart-experiment.sh}, \texttt{run/recorded-runs/autorestart/}).

\textbf{What the numbers show.} Every fault but one was detected in both systems, and the true cause was within the top three suspects in 65 to 71\,\% of the faults. The differences between the on and off rows are 9 and 15 points of Top-1, and the false-alarm column moves in opposite directions (more with auto-restart on in ShopFlow, fewer in LedgerLine). With 21 faults the intervals overlap completely, so none of these differences can be called an effect of auto-restart. Brownouts are the weak class in all four runs (no Top-1 at all), which agrees with the 62-fault results of Section~\ref{sec:resultsmain}.

\textbf{Why there was no effect to find.} The log of each run shows what auto-restart actually did. It restarted no container of the bench under test. The one restart in each ``on'' run was of the \emph{other} bench's message bus, which had been stopped by hand to keep one bench per run and which exited with code 1; the mapper, which sees every container on the host, restarted it. That is an artefact of the setup and is reported as such. Three properties of the mapper and the benches explain the rest:
\begin{itemize}
\item the benches set \texttt{restart: always}, so Docker brings a crashed container back within seconds, before the mapper's next pass;
\item the mapper only looks for dead containers once per discovery cycle (180\,s), longer than the 20-second crash faults;
\item it skips containers that exited with code 0, and a container stopped with \texttt{docker stop} exits with code 0.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{A direct test with long outages.} To give auto-restart a chance to act, \texttt{cart} and \texttt{payments} were each stopped for 5 minutes by the Fault Lab (\texttt{run/scripts/autorestart-stop-test.sh}), with the state of each container sampled every 2 seconds. Table~\ref{tab:stoptest} shows no difference: the mapper left the containers down in both modes.

\textbf{Conclusion.} On these benchmarks auto-restart is inert: it neither helped nor hurt detection or root-cause accuracy beyond run-to-run variation, and the rule that skips exit code 0 means a deliberate stop is never undone. It would matter on a host whose containers have no restart policy and exit with a failure code, which these benches do not model, so that case is untested. Switching it off for evaluation remains the safe default, because the mapper then cannot heal what the injector breaks.

\section{Analysis}
\textbf{What the evidence supports.} Three results are robust. The fact-aware hybrid names the true cause more often than the rules on the real systems (the paired interval excludes zero). The container facts are the single most valuable component (the ablation shows 19 and 16 points). And the learned propagation model discriminates better than fixed edge probabilities on observed cascades (AUROC 0.847 against 0.784).

\textbf{What did not work.} Three claims that were expected to hold did not. The random walk is not statistically better than ranking by anomaly alone on these benchmarks. Learned directions do not beat the name rule when names exist. Onset-precedence re-ranking was neutral. These are reported because a pipeline that is honest about where it stops is more useful to an operator than one that is not. The simple rule $z$-score is a strong detector on its own, and the Isolation Forest earns its place through quietness and complementarity, not discrimination.

\textbf{The measurement bug.} Before the ledger reader was fixed, every engine was scored on only part of the real faults (14 of 31 per system were hidden) and the outages of the hidden faults were counted as false alarms. That is why every number in this report uses all 62 faults, and why a regression test now guards the reader.

\section{Discussion and Threats to Validity}
\textbf{Construct validity.} Ground truth is the injector's ledger. It records the structural root cause and the services able to be affected; whether an affected service visibly changes depends on protection the ledger does not model, so cascade recall is a lower bound on true propagation. The evidence weighting of Section~\ref{sec:graph} is not isolated by the offline benchmarks because the recordings do not retain edge evidence.

\textbf{Internal validity.} Settings were chosen by the authors. On the synthetic track they were chosen on a disjoint tuning split and scored once; on the real track they were not tuned on a held-out system, so the real numbers are optimistic by an unmeasured margin. The two container facts were found by inspecting the real runs.

\textbf{External validity.} Two real applications and one capture. Architecture independence is argued from the cross-system check and the synthetic topologies, not proven; the simulator is cleaner than a real system (for example, restarts do not lower memory in it).

\textbf{Conclusion validity.} With 62 real faults a difference below about eight points cannot be resolved, and overlapping faults make the intervals optimistic.

\textbf{A caution about auto-restart.} The mapper can restart crashed containers on a target (Section~\ref{sec:tools} and Chapter~\ref{ch:system}). Section~\ref{sec:live} compares live campaigns with it on and off and finds no effect on these benches; evaluation runs are still best made with it switched off, so that the mapper cannot heal the faults the injector applies.

\section{Significance and Implications}
Three practical points follow. An instrumentation-free tool can recover a usable call graph and a useful ranking, and for small teams that is the difference between having a diagnosis tool and not. Container facts are worth more than any statistical refinement: a stopped container is something one knows, and folding it into an anomaly score throws information away. And the right operating contract for an alarm is the one the operator experiences, which is why alarm time is reported beside false-alarm episodes.

\section{Potential Applications}
\begin{itemize}
\item On-call support for small teams running Docker Compose stacks on a few hosts.
\item Teaching and labs on cascading failure, with a blind fault injector and a recorded-run format students can replay.
\item A first-line triage layer in front of heavier observability systems, giving a ranked suspect and a runbook.
\item Capacity and resilience reviews using the what-if and blast-radius views.
\item A benchmark harness for new detectors and rankers, with ground truth the detector never sees.
\end{itemize}
